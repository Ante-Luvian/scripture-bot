# ScriptureBot changelog

What's new, newest first. When the bot starts after an update, she posts any
entries a server hasn't seen yet to that server's changelog channel
(`!changelogsetup #channel`); anyone can read recent ones with `!changelog`.

Format: one `## YYYY-MM-DD · Title` section per notable change (new commands,
new features, fixes users would notice), with `- ` bullets written for users.
Once an entry has been announced, don't change its heading — the heading is
how the bot remembers what it has already posted.

Write every bullet for someone who has never seen the code or this project's
history (see CLAUDE.md): one change per bullet; say in plain words what's new
or what was wrong; give an example command to try; explain any term a casual
reader wouldn't know.

## 2026-11-11 · Every command, on every server
- ⚙️ **`!packs` is gone** (and `/setup packs`). It let admins switch off groups of commands for their server, like the Rosary, Jewish learning or divination. Now every server simply has every command, so nothing is ever missing. If your server had switched a group off, those commands work again, and they're back in the `/` menu.
- 🙏 **Nothing is held back any more.** A server that had chosen only some churches' commands didn't get the others' prayers (`!prayer hail mary` said it didn't know one), Latin chant in the hymn of the day, or every church's calendar in `!today`. Every server now gets all of them. Try `!prayer list`.

## 2026-11-11 · Joseph and Asenath: the longer version
- 💍 **The longer *Joseph and Asenath*** is now in the library, beside the shorter one we already had. This ancient Jewish romance (about the first century) tells how Asenath, the proud daughter of an Egyptian priest, falls for Joseph, smashes her idols and repents in sackcloth for seven days. An angel then visits her with a mysterious honeycomb, and in the second half Pharaoh's son plots to kill Joseph and carry her off. The longer version has the story in full, scenes the shorter one cuts. This is E. W. Brooks's 1918 translation, never typed up online before: it has been converted from a scan of the book and checked by eye against every page. Each of its 29 chapters starts with Brooks's own chapter title. Try `!verse Longer Asenath 16` for the honeycomb. In `!books` it sits beside the shorter version, under the Pseudepigrapha's legends and retellings of scripture.

## 2026-11-11 · Two more Qur'an commentaries
The **📖 Commentary** button under a Qur'an verse now has two more commentaries in its menu:
- **Asbab al-Nuzul** ("the occasions of revelation") by al-Wahidi, an 11th-century scholar. It tells the story behind a verse: what had happened, or who had asked what, when the verse was revealed. It covers only the verses it has a story for, so it appears under those verses alone. Try `!verse Quran 2:6`, then **📖 Commentary**, then pick it from the menu.
- **Tafsir al-Tustari**, by Sahl al-Tustari (died 896), one of the earliest Sufi (mystical) commentaries. It reads the verses for their inner, spiritual meaning. Try `!verse Quran 2:255`.

## 2026-11-11 · Three ancient texts, rescued from old scans
These had never been typed up anywhere online, only photographed in old library books. Each one has been converted to text and checked by eye against every page of the original.
- ✝️ **The Acts of Apollonius**, the trial of a Roman Christian put to death under the Emperor Commodus, around AD 185. Before the Senate he defends his faith in a speech that mocks the idols ("The Egyptians do homage to an onion in their folly") and answers the Prefect calmly to the end. It survives in an old Armenian translation, in F. C. Conybeare's English (1894). The verses follow the speech's numbered sections: try `!verse Acts of Apollonius 1:30`. It's in `!books` under ⛪ The Early Church → Early Church Documents, beside the Scillitan Martyrs.
- 📜 **The Testaments of Isaac and Jacob**, companions to the Testament of Abraham, preserved by the Egyptian (Coptic) Church. In each, an angel comes to tell the patriarch his death is near. Isaac is shown the punishments of the wicked and the joys of heaven before he dies, and Jacob blesses his sons and is carried to his fathers. Translated by Stephen Gaselee (1927). Try `!verse Testament of Isaac 1:1` or `!verse Testament of Jacob 1:1`. Both are in `!books` under the Pseudepigrapha's Testaments.

## 2026-11-11 · !pascal: Pascal's Pensées, one thought at a time
- 🕯️ **New command: `!pascal`** (or `!pensees`). Blaise Pascal was a 17th-century French mathematician who, near the end of his short life, filled notebooks with thoughts for a defence of Christianity he never finished. Published after his death as the *Pensées* ("Thoughts"), they are some of the most quoted lines ever written about faith, doubt and being human: "The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know."
  - `!pascal` gives a thought at random.
  - `!pascal 233` gives a thought by its number, the numbering everyone uses. 233 is his famous wager on God's existence.
  - `!pascal heart reason` finds a thought with those words in it.
  - **🖼️ Card** turns the thought into a shareable image, and **◀ ▶** step to the thoughts either side.
  - `/pascal` works too.

## 2026-11-11 · Qur'an commentary for everyone, and no more setup questions
- 📖 **The Qur'an commentary is now posted for everyone.** Pressing **📖 Commentary** under a Qur'an verse (try `!verse Quran 2:255`) used to show the commentary only to you. Now the whole channel sees it, so you can read and discuss it together.
- ◀ ▶ **New buttons to step to the verse before or after**, like **◀ 2:254** and **2:256 ▶**, so you can read the commentary verse by verse. The old ◀ ▶ arrows turned pages, so on a short commentary with only one page they were greyed out and seemed to do nothing. Now page buttons only appear when there are pages to turn. The buttons also keep working after the bot restarts.
- ⚙️ **`!serversetup` is gone**, along with `/setup traditions` and the "Set me up" button on the welcome message. It asked admins a series of questions about what their server was for. Everything is now simply on. Admins who want a tidier `/` menu can still switch off a group of commands with `!packs`, for example `!packs divination off`.

## 2026-11-11 · The whole library on every server
- 📚 **Every server now has every book.** Until now, a server's admins could pick which religions' texts the bot would show, and the rest were hidden from `!verse`, `!search`, `!books` and the rest (as if the bot didn't have them). That choice is gone: the whole library is open everywhere. If your server had picked only some traditions, you'll now also find the others, for example `!books buddhist` or `!verse Gita 2:47`.
- ⚙️ **Setting up a server still chooses its commands.** `!serversetup` (or `/setup traditions`) still asks what your server is for, and uses the answers to decide which extra commands to offer, like the Rosary, the Torah portion or the Islamic prayer times, so your `/` menu stays tidy. `!packs` (or `/setup packs`) still switches one group of commands on or off, for example `!packs divination off`. It no longer takes a tradition's name, since texts can't be switched off any more.

## 2026-11-11 · Saints and mystics: Anselm to Pascal
Twenty-three classics of Catholic Christianity are now in the library, from the Middle Ages to the 1600s. Each is the full text in a standard English translation, read with `!verse` like any other book. Look for them in `!books` under ✝️ Christianity.
- 🏰 **Medieval Christianity** has two new shelves and a fuller one:
  - **Theologians:** St. Anselm of Canterbury, the monk who tried to prove God exists by reason alone. His *Proslogium* has the famous "ontological argument" (that God is "that than which nothing greater can be conceived"): try `!verse Proslogium 2`. Also his *Monologium*, his *Cur Deus Homo* ("Why God Became Man", books 1 and 2 by chapter: `!verse Cur Deus Homo 1.1`), and the monk Gaunilo's reply *In Behalf of the Fool* with Anselm's answer. Plus St. Bonaventure's *The Mind's Road to God* (`!verse Bonaventure 1`).
  - **Franciscans:** St. Francis of Assisi's own writings: his Rules, his Testament, his letters and prayers, and the *Canticle of the Sun* (`!verse Writings of St. Francis 19`). And the *Little Flowers of St. Francis*, the much-loved old stories of Francis and his first friars, like the wolf he tamed (`!verse Little Flowers 21`), with the *Considerations on the Holy Stigmata*, the *Life of Friar Juniper* and the *Sayings of Friar Giles*.
  - **Mystics:** now with St. Bernard of Clairvaux's *On Loving God* (`!verse Bernard 1`), the sermons of Meister Eckhart (`!verse Eckhart 1`) and *The Dialogue* of St. Catherine of Siena (`!verse Catherine of Siena 1`).
- 🕯️ **Reformation and After → Catholic Writers**, the Catholic side of the 1500s and 1600s:
  - **The Council of Trent**, the Church's answer to the Protestant Reformation: all its canons and decrees, one session per chapter (`!verse Trent 6` is the decree on justification).
  - St. Ignatius of Loyola's **Spiritual Exercises**, the retreat manual of the Jesuits (`!verse Spiritual Exercises 7`).
  - St. Teresa of Ávila's **Interior Castle**, the soul as a castle of seven "mansions" leading to God, by mansion and chapter (`!verse Interior Castle 7.2`), and her **Life**, her own story (`!verse Teresa 1`).
  - St. John of the Cross: the **Ascent of Mount Carmel** and the **Dark Night of the Soul** by book and chapter (`!verse Dark Night 1.1`), and the **Spiritual Canticle**, his love poem of the soul and God, explained stanza by stanza (`!verse Spiritual Canticle 1`).
  - St. Francis de Sales's **Introduction to the Devout Life**, by part and chapter (`!verse Devout Life 1.1`).
  - Blaise Pascal's **Pensées** ("Thoughts"), by the numbers everyone cites them by: `!verse Pascal 233` is his famous wager on God's existence.
- Only the authors' own words are included; the translators' introductions and footnotes are left out.

## 2026-11-10 · A new map for the whole library
The library in `!books` has been rearranged so that every book sits with others of its kind, and every faith sits where its own followers would put it. Nothing has been removed: all the same books are there, and every `!verse` reference works as before. The main menu is now ✝️ Christianity · ✡️ Judaism · ☪️ Islamic · ✴️ Bahá'í · 🧿 Gnostic & Esoteric · 🏛️ Classical · 🌙 Ancient Near East & Iran · 🪷 Dharmic & Asian · 🌍 Folk Myth & Fairy Stories · 📜 English Literature.
- ✡️ **Judaism, by era.** Four shelves instead of a grab bag:
  - 📜 **Second Temple Judaism:** the writings from roughly 300 BC to AD 100. That covers the **Pseudepigrapha** (Jewish books written under the names of ancient figures like Enoch and Abraham), now sorted the way the standard modern edition sorts them: apocalypses, testaments, legends and retellings of scripture, wisdom, and psalms and prayers. It also has the **Dead Sea Scrolls**, sorted by kind (community rules, hymns, commentaries, Bible manuscripts), plus Philo and Josephus. Try `!books pseudepigrapha`.
  - 📚 **Rabbinic Literature:** the Mishnah, Talmuds, Midrash and Targums.
  - ✨ **Jewish Mysticism:** the Zohar and Kabbalah, and the Hekhalot texts, the earliest Jewish mystical writings about ascents to God's heavenly palaces (`!books jewish mysticism`).
  - 🕍 **Medieval Judaism:** Maimonides and other medieval works.
- ✴️ **The Bahá'í Faith now has its own place** in the main menu instead of sharing one with Islam. Bahá'ís see their faith as a religion of its own, and Muslims agree it isn't part of Islam (`!books baha'i`).
- ☪️ **Islam** opens straight to its books, now grouped as Quran, Hadith, Life of the Prophet, Sufism (al-Ghazali, Hujwiri and the Persian poets), Shi'ite, Isma'ili and other writings.
- 🔥 **Iranian Religions:** the Zoroastrian scriptures (the Avesta and the later Persian books) and the **Yazidi** texts now share a shelf, under 🌙 Ancient Near East & Iran. Yazidism has deep roots in old Iranian religion (`!books iranian religions`).
- The **Mandaeans**, the one ancient Gnostic religion still practised today, stay with the 🧿 Gnostics. **Emanuel Swedenborg**, whose followers form a Christian church, moved from the magic shelf to ✝️ Christianity → Reformation and After.
- 🔮 **Magic & Esotericism** is regrouped: the Hermetica (the Greek writings attributed to Hermes Trismegistus), Renaissance magic (Ficino, Pico, Agrippa, John Dee), grimoires and Solomonic magic, the magical papyri, alchemy and astrology.
- 🏛️ **Long author lists are easier to browse.** Plutarch's *Parallel Lives* now come in his own pairs (each Greek life with the Roman life he matched it with, and his comparison of the two), and his essays, the *Moralia*, by subject. Lucian is sorted by subject, and Demosthenes by kind of speech (to the Assembly, the great public trials, private lawsuits). Try `!books plutarch` or `!books demosthenes`.
- 🕉️ The Hindu scriptures' shelf is now called **Hindu** (it used to be "Dharmic"), and the ballads and fables moved to 🌍 Folk Myth & Fairy Stories.
- **Fixed:** searching `!books` for some authors whose works sit in groups found only part of them, or the wrong author's works. `!books swedenborg` and `!books maimonides` now show everything we have, and `!books plutarch` shows all of Plutarch (it would have shown only three works by a later imitator). The seven pre-Islamic Arabic odes (the Mu'allaqat) also appeared twice on their shelf; now they're listed once.

## 2026-11-10 · The earliest life of the Prophet Muhammad
- 🕌 **Ibn Ishaq's Life of the Prophet** (*Sirat Rasul Allah*), the oldest surviving biography of Muhammad, written in Medina and Baghdad in the 700s and kept in the edition of Ibn Hisham. This is Edward Rehatsek's English, the first ever made (finished in 1891), in Michael Edwardes's shortened version, in 26 chapters from Muhammad's birth to his death: the marriage to Khadija, the Night Journey on the winged beast Buraq (`!verse Sira 5:1`), the Hijra to Medina, the battle of Badr (`!verse Sira 13:1`), the conquest of Mecca and his last illness. `!verse Ibn Ishaq 1` works too, and it's in `!books` under ☪️ Islamic → Other Islamic Writings.

## 2026-11-10 · More hadith, the Stories of the Prophets, and commentary on every verse of the Quran
- 📜 **Five more hadith collections.** Hadith are the reports of what the Prophet Muhammad said and did, the second source of Islamic teaching after the Quran. Alongside Bukhari, Muslim and the other ten already here, you can now read:
  - **Riyad as-Salihin** ("Gardens of the Righteous"), al-Nawawi's selection of 1,896 hadith on living well, probably the hadith book Muslims read most: `!verse Riyad as-Salihin 1` is "deeds are judged by intentions";
  - **al-Adab al-Mufrad**, al-Bukhari's book on manners: kindness to parents, neighbours, guests (`!verse Al-Adab al-Mufrad 1`);
  - the **Shama'il**, al-Tirmidhi's portrait of the Prophet: how he looked, dressed, ate and laughed (`!verse Shama'il Muhammadiyah 1`);
  - **Bulugh al-Maram** and **Mishkat al-Masabih**, two classic handbooks gathering the hadith on each part of Muslim life (`!verse Mishkat al-Masabih 1`).
  They're numbered as on sunnah.com, so a number you find cited there works here. Browse them in `!books` under ☪️ Islamic → Hadith.
- 📖 **A Commentary button under every Quran verse.** Look up a verse, such as `!verse Quran 2:255` (the Throne Verse), and press 📖 **Commentary** to see what the classical commentators said about it. It opens on the *Tafsir al-Jalalayn*, a short verse-by-verse explanation written in 15th-century Cairo, and a menu switches to **Ibn Kathir** (the most widely read commentary, long, with the hadith it draws on) or the commentary handed down under the name of **Ibn 'Abbas**, the Prophet's cousin. Only you see the reply, so it doesn't fill the channel.
- 🌟 **Ibn Kathir's Stories of the Prophets**, the 14th-century retelling of the lives of 29 prophets, from Adam and Noah through Abraham, Joseph, Moses, David and Jesus to Muhammad, drawn from the Quran and the hadith. A chapter for each prophet: `!verse Stories of the Prophets 12:1` begins Joseph's story. It's in `!books` under ☪️ Islamic → Other Islamic Writings.

## 2026-11-10 · The Kebra Nagast, the Cave of Treasures and the Book of the Bee
Three books that Eastern Christians have treasured for centuries alongside the Bible, all in E. A. Wallis Budge's translations. None of them has verse numbers in print, so each verse here is one of Budge's paragraphs, or a few sentences of a long one.
- 👑 **The Kebra Nagast** ("The Glory of Kings"), Ethiopia's national epic, written down in the 1300s. The Queen of Sheba visits King Solomon, returns home carrying his son, and that son, Bayna-Leḥkem (better known as Menelik), later goes to Jerusalem and brings the Ark of the Covenant back to Ethiopia, where the Ethiopian Church believes it still is. Ethiopia's emperors traced their line to him. Try `!verse Kebra Nagast 21` for the Queen of Sheba and `!verse Kebra Nagast 48` for the taking of the Ark. It's in `!books` under 👼 Apocrypha / Pseudepigrapha → "Ethiopian Orthodox Canon". `!verse Glory of Kings 32:2` works too.
- 💎 **The Cave of Treasures**, a Syriac history of the world from Creation to Pentecost, from around the 500s. It retells Genesis with legends of its own: Adam and Eve live in a cave on the mountain of Paradise, the patriarchs' bodies are kept there, and Noah's son Shem carries Adam's body to Golgotha, so that Christ is later crucified over Adam's grave (`!verse Cave of Treasures 3:27-28`). Its six chapters are its own six periods: the five thousand years from Adam, and the five hundred from Cyrus to Christ.
- 🐝 **The Book of the Bee**, a collection of Bible history and legend "gathered like honey" by Solomon, a bishop of the Church of the East, about 1222. It follows the world from Creation to the Last Judgement, with stories found nowhere in the Bible, like the history of the staff Moses carried, which came from the Garden of Eden (`!verse Book of the Bee 30:1-2`), or the prophet Zoroaster foretelling Christ's birth (`!verse Book of the Bee 37:1-3`). Chapter 0 is Solomon's own preface.
- Budge's footnotes and, in the Cave of Treasures, the commentary he printed between the paragraphs are left out, so you get only the text itself. The Cave and the Bee are in `!books` under 👼 Apocrypha / Pseudepigrapha → "Pseudepigrapha".

## 2026-11-10 · Six more Jewish books from around the time of Jesus
Six books written by Jews (and in places touched up by Christians) in the centuries around the time of Jesus, retelling or expanding the stories of the Old Testament. None of them made it into any Bible, but they were read widely, and they show what people believed about angels, the end of the world and the afterlife. All six are in `!books` under 👼 Apocrypha / Pseudepigrapha → "Pseudepigrapha".
- 💍 **Joseph and Aseneth.** Genesis says in one line that Joseph married Aseneth, daughter of an Egyptian priest. This romance asks how a holy man could marry an idol-worshipper: Aseneth, proud and scornful, falls in love with Joseph at first sight, smashes her idols, fasts and repents in sackcloth for seven days, and is visited by an angel who feeds her a honeycomb that bees from paradise have made (`!verse Aseneth 16:14`). Her prayer of repentance is `!verse Aseneth 12`. Translated by David Cook (1984).
- 🔥 **The Apocalypse of Abraham.** The young Abraham works in his father's idol shop, sees the gods break and burn, and works out that they can't be gods at all. Then an angel named Jaoel takes him up to heaven on the wings of a bird to see God's throne of fire, the world laid out below him, and the fall of Adam and Eve. Try the hymn the angel teaches him: `!verse Apocalypse of Abraham 17`. Translated by G. H. Box (1918), with the "thee"s and "thou"s modernised. Box printed it without verse numbers, so here a verse is a paragraph, or a few sentences of a long one.
- 🪶 **The Testament of Job.** Job, dying, tells his children his story: how he destroyed a temple of idols, how Satan came disguised as a beggar and then as a bread-seller, and how his wife Sitis sold her hair to buy him bread. It ends with his three daughters receiving girdles from heaven that let them speak the language of angels (`!verse Testament of Job 11:26-28`), and has a hymn sung by his friend Eliphaz (`!verse Testament of Job 10:13-23`). Translated by Kaufmann Kohler (1897), lightly modernised.
- 📜 **The Lives of the Prophets.** Short notes on 23 prophets: where each came from, how he died and where he is buried, with a few legends besides. Isaiah was sawn in two; Jeremiah, stoned in Egypt, hid the Ark of the Covenant in a cliff (`!verse Lives of the Prophets 2`). A chapter for each prophet, adapted from Charles Cutler Torrey's translation (1946).
- 🎺 **The Apocalypse of Zephaniah.** The prophet Zephaniah is taken up by an angel to see the dead: the angels who write down every good and bad deed, a sea of fire, and the scroll on which all his own sins are written (`!verse Apocalypse of Zephaniah 2:1-5`). Pages of the only manuscript are missing, which the text says where it happens. Chapters 1 to 12 are the main Coptic text; chapter 13 is the passage quoted by the early Christian writer Clement of Alexandria, and chapter 14 a separate scrap of papyrus. Translated by O. S. Wintermute (1983).
- 🐉 **The Apocalypse of Elijah.** A prophecy of the end: wars of kings in Egypt, then the "son of lawlessness", who works every miracle Christ did except raising the dead, until Elijah and Enoch come down to defeat him (`!verse Apocalypse of Elijah 32`). Translated from the Coptic by Anthony Alcock (2013). The verse numbers are the page numbers of the printed Coptic text (19 to 43), so it starts at `!verse Apocalypse of Elijah 19`.

## 2026-11-10 · The Gospel of Marcion, the first Christian Bible's only gospel
- 📜 **The Gospel of Marcion**, reconstructed. Marcion was a shipowner from the Black Sea coast who came to Rome about the year 140 and taught that the Father of Jesus was not the God of the Old Testament but a higher, unknown God of pure kindness. The Church expelled him in 144, and he founded churches of his own that lasted for centuries. He was also the first person we know of to draw up a fixed list of Christian scripture: ten letters of Paul and a single gospel, with no Old Testament at all.
- That gospel is lost. But the writers who attacked him, above all Tertullian and Epiphanius, quoted it line by line to refute it, and from their quotations scholars have pieced most of it back together. It is close to the Gospel of Luke, only shorter, and keeps Luke's chapter and verse numbers so the two can be compared. This is J. H. Hill's reconstruction (1891), revised by Daniel Mahar to follow the German scholar Theodor Zahn.
- What to look for:
  - **It has no birth story, no John the Baptist and no baptism.** There are no chapters 1 to 3; Jesus simply comes down from heaven as a grown man: `!verse Gospel of Marcion 4:31`.
  - **The Lord's Prayer asks for the Spirit:** "Father, let thy Holy Spirit come upon us" (`!verse Gospel of Marcion 11:2-4`).
  - **A famous saying is turned around.** In Luke not one tittle of *the law* can fail; here it is "one tittle of *my words*" (`!verse Gospel of Marcion 16:16-17`).
  - **The risen Jesus still has flesh and bones** (`!verse Gospel of Marcion 24:39`), a verse Marcion's opponents made much of, since he taught that Christ's body only seemed real.
- How to read it: gaps in the verse numbers are verses nobody quotes, so we can't tell what Marcion had there. Words in square brackets are less certain, and *(Zahn omits)* marks a passage Zahn thought was not in Marcion's gospel at all (`!verse Gospel of Marcion 5:15`). `!verse Marcion's Gospel 6:20-23` works too.
- It's in `!books` under 🧿 Gnostic & Esoteric → 🧿 Gnostic → "Marcion (Reconstructed)".

## 2026-11-10 · Lost gospels in fragments, and five early church documents
- 📜 **Lost gospels that survive only in pieces.** The early Church knew gospels besides the four in the Bible. Most are lost, and all we have is what early writers quoted from them or scraps of papyrus dug up in Egypt. Here are those pieces, most in M. R. James's translation (1924), under 👼 Apocrypha / Pseudepigrapha → "Lost Gospels in Fragments":
  - the **Gospel according to the Hebrews**, used by Jewish Christians, where Jesus calls the Holy Spirit "my mother" (`!verse Gospel of the Hebrews 5`), with the **Gospel of the Nazoreans** and the **Gospel of the Ebionites**, two related Jewish-Christian gospels;
  - the **Gospel according to the Egyptians**, with Jesus's sayings to Salome: "When ye have trampled on the garment of shame…" (`!verse Gospel of the Egyptians 3`);
  - the **Egerton Gospel**, papyrus scraps from the 100s in the British Library, with a healing of a leper told differently from the Bible's (`!verse Egerton Gospel 2`);
  - two papyri found at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt: **840**, where Jesus argues with a priest in the Temple about washing (`!verse Oxyrhynchus 840 3`), and **1224**;
  - the **Fayoum fragment**, a few lines on Jesus foretelling Peter's denial (`!verse Fayoum 1`), and the **Preaching of Peter**, an early defence of the faith (`!verse Preaching of Peter 4`).

## 2026-11-02 · The Revelations of Methodius, in Middle English verse
- 🔥 ***The Revelations of Methodius***, a poem of 975 lines in the English of the 1400s. ## 2026-10-26 · More medieval English: the N-Town plays, the Tudor poets, the Scots makars
- 🎭 **The N-Town plays, a whole medieval cycle of Bible plays.** In the 1400s English towns acted out the story of the world, from the Creation to the Last Judgement, in a chain of short plays put on by ordinary townspeople at the summer feast of Corpus Christi. This cycle of 42 plays (once called the *Ludus Coventriae*, "the Coventry plays") has Noah, Abraham and Isaac, the Nativity, the Passion and Doomsday, with a Prologue that tells the audience what's coming. They are cited by play and stanza: `!verse Ludus Coventriae 15:1` is the start of the Nativity play, where Joseph grumbles about the emperor's tax ("Lord, what travayl to man is wrought!"), and `!verse Ludus Coventriae 0:1` is the Prologue.
- 🐦 **John Skelton, Henry VIII's tutor and the wildest poet of his day.** He wrote in short, galloping rhyming lines that are still called "Skeltonics". Here are *Phyllyp Sparowe*, a mock funeral for a girl's pet sparrow killed by a cat (`!verse Phyllyp Sparowe 1`); *Elynour Rummynge*, about an alewife and her rowdy customers; *Magnyfycence*, a play about a prince ruined by bad advisers; and his attacks on Cardinal Wolsey (*Colyn Cloute*, *Speke, Parrot*, *Why Come Ye Nat to Courte*), with his shorter poems. Like the other medieval poems they are cited by line number. (His poems in Latin are left out.)
- 🏰 **Stephen Hawes**, poet at the court of Henry VII: *The Pastime of Pleasure*, a long allegory in which the knight Graunde Amoure studies the seven liberal arts on his way to win the lady La Bell Pucell (`!verse Pastime of Pleasure 1:1`), and *The Example of Vertu* and three shorter poems, from the first printed editions of about 1500–1510.
- 🏴 **The Scots poets of the 1500s** (the *makars*, as Scotland called its poets): Sir David Lyndsay (`!verse Lyndsay's Poems 1:1` is his *Dreme*), Alexander Montgomerie's *Cherrie and the Slae* (`!verse Montgomerie's Poems 1:1`), and poems by King James V, John Bellenden, Sir Richard Maitland and Alexander Scott, in the Scots they wrote in.
- 🚢 **Two oddities:** *Cocke Lorelles Bote*, a comic poem of about 1510 in which a crew of rogues and tradesmen sets sail in a ship of fools, and John Lydgate's *Disguising at Hertford*, a little entertainment put on for the boy king Henry VI on New Year's Eve, where henpecked husbands complain to the king about their wives.

## 2026-10-25 · Medieval English poetry, in the words its poets wrote
- 📜 **A new part of the library: medieval English poetry, about 80 poems.** These are the poems of England and Scotland from about 1250 to 1500, in *Middle English*: the English of the time, before spelling was settled. It reads like a cousin of today's English ("Whan that Aprille with his shoures soote…"), and uses two old letters, **þ** ("thorn", said *th*) and **ȝ** ("yogh", said *y* or *gh*), kept here as the poets wrote them. The texts come from scholarly editions of 1856–1921 and the National Library of Scotland. `!books` → Medieval English poetry lists them all, and `!verse Canterbury Tales 1:1` is a good place to start.
- ✒️ **Geoffrey Chaucer, nearly all of him.** The *Canterbury Tales*, the pilgrims' story-telling contest on the road to Canterbury; *Troilus and Criseyde*, his long love story set in the siege of Troy; the dream poems (*The Book of the Duchess*, *The House of Fame*, *The Parliament of Fowls*, where the birds meet on St Valentine's day to choose their mates); *The Legend of Good Women*; *The Romaunt of the Rose*; and his short poems (*Truth*, *Gentilesse*, the *Complaint to his Purse*…). Also the poets who followed him, once printed with his works: Robert Henryson's *Testament of Cresseid*, which finishes Criseyde's story, and poems by John Lydgate, Thomas Hoccleve and others. Try `!verse Troilus 3:1` or `!verse Parliament of Fowls 1`.
- 🌾 **The other great poems of the 1300s.** *Piers Plowman*, William Langland's dream vision of England and the search for how to live well; *Pearl*, where a grieving father meets his little daughter in a dream of heaven, with *Cleanness* and *Patience* (the story of Jonah), all by the unknown poet who wrote *Sir Gawain*; and John Gower's *Confessio Amantis* ("A Lover's Confession"), a lover confessing his sins, told in more than a hundred stories.
- ⚔️ **Romances, and a whole medieval book.** The old adventure stories: *Sir Orfeo* (the Greek tale of Orpheus, retold as a fairy story with a happy ending), *Havelok the Dane*, *King Horn* and others. And the poems of the **Auchinleck manuscript**, a big book of poems copied in London around 1335 and now in the National Library of Scotland: romances like *Sir Beues of Hamtoun* and *Guy of Warwick*, saints' lives, a verse history of England, and short comic and moral poems.
- 🏴 **Scotland's two great epics**, in Scots: John Barbour's *Bruce* (1375), the story of Robert the Bruce and the battle of Bannockburn (`!verse Bruce 1:1`), and Blind Hary's *Wallace*, about William Wallace. Also two medieval plays (Noah and his family from Wakefield, Christ breaking open hell from York), a verse *Genesis and Exodus*, a book of medieval lyrics (`!verse Middle English Lyrics 3:1` is *Alysoun*) and political poems, and Edmund Spenser's *Faerie Queene* from Elizabeth I's reign.
- 🔢 **These poems are quoted by their line numbers**, the way scholars cite them, so you can look up a line you've seen quoted. `!verse Sir Orfeo 233` shows the passage that holds line 233, and `!verse Sir Orfeo 233-260` a stretch of lines. A poem in several books takes the book first: `!verse Troilus 3:1` is the start of Book III, and `!verse Confessio Amantis 0:1` the start of the Prologue.
- 📖 **Every book that is all one chapter is now cited by verse alone.** Short books with only one chapter used to be shown as "chapter 1": Jude 3 came back as "Jude 1:3". Now `!verse Jude 3`, `!verse Jude 3-5` and `!verse Obadiah 4` are shown as "Jude 3", "Jude 3–5" and "Obadiah 4", the way they're usually written, and the same goes for every one-chapter work in the library (Philemon, 2 and 3 John, Milton's *Samson Agonistes*, Hesiod's *Works and Days*…). ## 2026-10-25 · Medieval beasts: the Physiologus, and Reynard the Fox
- 🦁 **New command, `!physiologus` (or `!beast`): the Physiologus.** This is the little Christian book of animals, written in Greek in the early centuries of the Church, that lies behind every medieval bestiary and much of the animal symbolism in old churches and paintings. For each creature it tells what it was believed to do, and then what that teaches about Christ and the soul: the lioness whose cubs are born dead and wake when the lion breathes on them on the third day, the pelican that revives its young with its own blood, the phoenix that rises from its ashes. - 🦊 **New command, `!reynard` (or `!fox`): Reynard the Fox.** The great medieval story of the cunning fox: summoned to the court of King Noble the Lion to answer for his crimes, Reynard tricks Bruin the Bear, Tybert the Cat and Isegrim the Wolf, talks his way off the gallows, and gets away with nearly everything. It was a favourite all over medieval Europe, and this is William Caxton's English translation of 1481, one of the first books printed in England, in Henry Morley's edition with modern spelling. ## 2026-10-13 · Measures: dollars, spans, and Judas's thirty pieces of silver
- 💵 **`!measure` now gives Bible money in today's dollars too, as well as in days' wages.** In 1897 *Easton's Bible Dictionary* valued the denarius (the Gospels' "penny") at 7½d. to 8d. in British money of the time. Adjusted for inflation since then, that's about $7 today. `!measure 10 pence` or `!measure Luke 19:13` shows both. The two measures don't agree: a day's wage today is far more than $7, because Easton's priced the coin by its silver, not by what it would buy. So treat the dollar figure as a curiosity and the days' wages as the better guide.
- 🪙 **Judas's "thirty pieces of silver" are now converted.** `!measure Matthew 26:15` counts them as shekels, about 467 g of silver, worth about 120 days' wages. Matthew's Greek just says "silvers", but Matthew 27:9 ties them to the prophecy of thirty pieces of silver in Zechariah 11:12–13, and those were shekels. The "pieces of silver" in Luke 15 (the lost coin) and Acts 19:19 are drachmas, each worth about a denarius.
- ⚖️ **Silver weighed out as money now shows its value as well as its weight:** shekels of silver, talents of silver, and the talents in the parable (`!measure Matthew 25:15`, where five talents comes to about 60,000 days' wages).
- 📏 **Three units that were missing now work.** The **span** is the width of a spread hand, about 26 cm or half a cubit: `!measure 1 Samuel 17:4` gives Goliath's height, "six cubits and a span". The **day's journey** is 16 to 20 miles, as in "three days' journey into the wilderness" (`!measure Exodus 3:18`). The **dram** is a Persian gold coin, the daric, worth about a guinea (21 shillings) in 1897 (`!measure 1 Chronicles 29:7`).

## 2026-10-13 · Bible measures in today's terms
- 📏 **New: `!measure`, which turns Bible weights, measures and money into today's units.** The Bible measures in cubits, shekels, talents, ephahs and pence, and it's hard to picture what "an hundred talents" or "an omer for every man" means. `!measure talent` tells you what a talent was (the largest weight, 3,000 shekels), roughly what it weighs today (about 47 kg, or 103 lb), the verse that shows how many shekels it held, and a verse that uses it.
- 🔢 **Convert any amount:** `!measure 300 cubits` gives about 154 metres (506 feet). You can write the number the Bible's way too: `!measure three hundred cubits`, `!measure half a shekel`.
- 🚢 **Every measurement in a verse at once:** `!measure Genesis 6:15` reads the verse and converts each measurement in it, here the length, width and height of Noah's ark. - 🪙 **Money is given in days' wages, not today's prices.** A "penny" in the Gospels is a denarius, a labourer's pay for a day (Matthew 20:2). So `!measure Luke 19:13` shows that the "ten pounds" in the parable of the pounds came to about 1,000 days' wages, and the widow's two mites to 1/40 of a day's wage.
- 📚 **Where the numbers come from:** the sizes are the estimates of *Easton's Bible Dictionary* (1897), quoted with each unit. Measures varied from place to place and century to century, and scholars still disagree, so take them as rough guides. Where Easton's gives two different figures, both are shown. Some KJV words mean different things in different books: a "pound" is a weight of 60 shekels in Kings, a Roman pound of ointment in John, and a sum of money in Luke. Bare `!measure` lists all 30 units.
