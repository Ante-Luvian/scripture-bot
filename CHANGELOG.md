# ScriptureBot changelog

What's new, newest first. When the bot starts after an update, she posts any
entries a server hasn't seen yet to that server's changelog channel
(`!changelogsetup #channel`); anyone can read recent ones with `!changelog`.

Format: one `## YYYY-MM-DD · Title` section per notable change (new commands,
new features, fixes users would notice), with `- ` bullets written for users.
Once an entry has been announced, don't change its heading — the heading is
how the bot remembers what it has already posted.

Write every bullet for someone who has never seen the code or this project's
history (see CLAUDE.md): one change per bullet; say in plain words what's new
or what was wrong; give an example command to try; explain any term a casual
reader wouldn't know.

## 2026-10-13 · Seven new traditions and thirty new scriptures
- 📚 **The library has grown by about 30 sacred texts, and seven religions and traditions it didn't have before.** Every one works with `!verse`, `!search`, `!theme` (one passage on a subject from each tradition), `!discover`, `!card` and `!books`. Most are the classic translations of the *Sacred Books of the East*, the 50-volume series edited by Max Müller in the 1880s and 90s.
- 🪯 **Sikhism:** the whole **Guru Granth Sahib**, the Sikh scripture, cited by page (*Ang*) and line just as Sikhs cite it: `!verse Guru Granth Sahib 1:1`. Also the **Japji**, Guru Nanak's morning prayer that opens it, by stanza: `!verse Japji 1`.
- 🤚 **Jainism**, the ancient Indian religion of non-violence: the **Akaranga Sutra** (`!verse Akaranga Sutra 1.1.1`), the **Sutrakritanga**, the **Uttaradhyayana Sutra** (Mahavira's last teachings) and the **Kalpa Sutra**, the lives of the Jain teachers.
- ⛩️ **Shinto:** the **Kojiki** (`!verse Kojiki 1`), Japan's oldest book, its myths of the gods and the first emperors, and selections from the **Nihongi**, the Chronicles of Japan.
- 🔥 **Zoroastrianism**, the religion of ancient Persia, which had only Zarathustra's **Gathas** before: now also the **Yasna** (the worship service), the **Yashts** (hymns to the divine beings), the **Vendidad** (`!verse Vendidad 2`, the story of Yima's shelter from the great winter) and the **Bundahishn**, the Zoroastrian account of creation.
- 🌽 **The Maya:** the **Popol Vuh**, the K'iche' Maya book of creation, in Lewis Spence's retelling: `!verse Popol Vuh 1`.
- 🦢 ☘️ **Old Europe:** the Finnish epic, the **Kalevala** (`!verse Kalevala 1`), and the medieval Welsh tales of the **Mabinogion**, including some of the earliest stories of King Arthur (`!verse Mabinogion 1`).
- 🕉️ **More Hinduism:** nine more **Upanishads** in Max Müller's translation, among them the Chandogya and the Brihadaranyaka (`!verse Chandogya Upanishad 6.8:7`, "thou art it", the famous "Thou art that"); the **Yoga Sutras** of Patanjali (`!verse Yoga Sutras 1:2`); and the **Laws of Manu**.
- ☸️ **More Buddhism:** all 547 **Jataka** tales, the stories of the Buddha's former lives (`!verse Jataka 7`); the **Questions of King Milinda**, a Greek king's dialogue with a Buddhist monk; and the **Lotus Sutra**, the most loved scripture of East Asian Buddhism.
- ☯️ **More of China:** the **Zhuangzi**, the second great Daoist book (`!verse Zhuangzi 2` ends with the butterfly dream), and the rest of the Confucian Four Books: **Mencius** (by book, part and chapter: `!verse Mencius 6.1.2`), the **Great Learning** and the **Doctrine of the Mean**.
- 🌹 **Persian poetry:** Rumi's **Masnavi**, by book and story (`!verse Masnavi 1.0`, the song of the reed flute), and Omar Khayyam's **Rubaiyat** in FitzGerald's translation (`!verse Rubaiyat 1:12`, "A Book of Verses underneath the Bough").
- ✝️ **Christian mystics:** **The Cloud of Unknowing**, Julian of Norwich's **Revelations of Divine Love** (`!verse Julian of Norwich 5:1`) and Brother Lawrence's **Practice of the Presence of God**.
- 🔎 **Search one tradition** with `in:`: `!search compassion in:jain`, `in:sikh`, `in:shinto`, `in:zoroastrian`, `in:mesoamerican`, `in:finnish`, `in:celtic`. `!quiz jain` and `!quiz zoroastrian` are new quizzes, and `!quiz hard` now draws on the new books too.
- 📝 **A note on the old translations:** some of these copies have small gaps where a chapter was lost when the book was put online. Mencius is missing a few short sayings, and the *Nihongi* is a selection. Where that happens, the chapters keep their usual numbers, so a reference you look up elsewhere still lands in the right place here.

## 2026-10-13 · Measures: dollars, spans, and Judas's thirty pieces of silver
- 💵 **`!measure` now gives Bible money in today's dollars too, as well as in days' wages.** In 1897 *Easton's Bible Dictionary* valued the denarius (the Gospels' "penny") at 7½d. to 8d. in British money of the time. Adjusted for inflation since then, that's about $7 today. `!measure 10 pence` or `!measure Luke 19:13` shows both. The two measures don't agree: a day's wage today is far more than $7, because Easton's priced the coin by its silver, not by what it would buy. So treat the dollar figure as a curiosity and the days' wages as the better guide.
- 🪙 **Judas's "thirty pieces of silver" are now converted.** `!measure Matthew 26:15` counts them as shekels, about 467 g of silver, worth about 120 days' wages. Matthew's Greek just says "silvers", but Matthew 27:9 ties them to the prophecy of thirty pieces of silver in Zechariah 11:12–13, and those were shekels. The "pieces of silver" in Luke 15 (the lost coin) and Acts 19:19 are drachmas, each worth about a denarius.
- ⚖️ **Silver weighed out as money now shows its value as well as its weight:** shekels of silver, talents of silver, and the talents in the parable (`!measure Matthew 25:15`, where five talents comes to about 60,000 days' wages).
- 📏 **Three units that were missing now work.** The **span** is the width of a spread hand, about 26 cm or half a cubit: `!measure 1 Samuel 17:4` gives Goliath's height, "six cubits and a span". The **day's journey** is 16 to 20 miles, as in "three days' journey into the wilderness" (`!measure Exodus 3:18`). The **dram** is a Persian gold coin, the daric, worth about a guinea (21 shillings) in 1897 (`!measure 1 Chronicles 29:7`).

## 2026-10-13 · Bible measures in today's terms
- 📏 **New: `!measure`, which turns Bible weights, measures and money into today's units.** The Bible measures in cubits, shekels, talents, ephahs and pence, and it's hard to picture what "an hundred talents" or "an omer for every man" means. `!measure talent` tells you what a talent was (the largest weight, 3,000 shekels), roughly what it weighs today (about 47 kg, or 103 lb), the verse that shows how many shekels it held, and a verse that uses it.
- 🔢 **Convert any amount:** `!measure 300 cubits` gives about 154 metres (506 feet). You can write the number the Bible's way too: `!measure three hundred cubits`, `!measure half a shekel`.
- 🚢 **Every measurement in a verse at once:** `!measure Genesis 6:15` reads the verse and converts each measurement in it, here the length, width and height of Noah's ark. Try `!measure Exodus 38:25` for the silver given for the tabernacle, or `!measure John 2:6` for the water jars at the wedding at Cana.
- 🪙 **Money is given in days' wages, not today's prices.** A "penny" in the Gospels is a denarius, a labourer's pay for a day (Matthew 20:2). So `!measure Luke 19:13` shows that the "ten pounds" in the parable of the pounds came to about 1,000 days' wages, and the widow's two mites to 1/40 of a day's wage.
- 📚 **Where the numbers come from:** the sizes are the estimates of *Easton's Bible Dictionary* (1897), quoted with each unit. Measures varied from place to place and century to century, and scholars still disagree, so take them as rough guides. Where Easton's gives two different figures, both are shown. Some KJV words mean different things in different books: a "pound" is a weight of 60 shekels in Kings, a Roman pound of ointment in John, and a sum of money in Luke. Bare `!measure` lists all 30 units.

## 2026-10-13 · Versle: the daily verse puzzle
- 📜 **New game: `!versle`, a verse puzzle for every day, a bit like Wordle.** Everyone gets the same well-known verse each day. The puzzle is to say where in the Bible it is, and you have six guesses. Press **✍️ Guess** and type a verse, like `John 3:16`.
- 🟩 **After each guess you get a row of squares and a hint, shown only to you.** The three squares are for the book, the chapter and the verse: 🟩 means right, 🟨 means within three, ⬛ means further off. The hint says which way to go, such as "later in the Bible (by 5 books) — in the New Testament" or "Right book! Chapter: earlier (by 2 chapters)".
- 📣 **Share without spoilers:** when you finish, **Share my result** posts your rows of squares in the channel (like "Versle #1 3/6"), but not the answer. `!versle stats` shows how many you've solved and your streak of days in a row.
- A new verse comes every day at midnight UTC. The verses are ones people know and look up most, so you'll often recognise them: well over a year of puzzles before any repeats.

## 2026-10-13 · The four Gospels side by side
- 📖 **New: `!harmony` shows one event in the life of Jesus as each Gospel tells it, side by side.** Matthew, Mark, Luke and John often tell the same story, but each puts in or leaves out different details. Seeing them together shows what each one adds. Try `!harmony feeding five thousand`, the one miracle all four Gospels record. A line under the title says who tells each story ("Told by Matthew, Mark and Luke", "Only in John").
- 🧭 **Walk through the whole life of Christ.** The events follow a *harmony of the Gospels*, a scholar's arrangement of all four accounts into one timeline: A. T. Robertson's of 1922, which has 185 events from the birth of John the Baptist to the Ascension. **◀ Event before** and **Event after ▶** step through them in order, and a button under each passage reads that Gospel's account in full.
- 🔎 **Find an event** by words from it (`!harmony transfiguration`, `!harmony lazarus`), by a verse (`!harmony Mark 6:41` finds the event that verse belongs to), or by its number (`!harmony 72`). `!harmony` on its own picks one at random.

## 2026-10-13 · Journeys on a map
- 🧭 **New: `!journey` draws the great journeys of the Bible on a map.** The stops are numbered and joined by arrows so you can follow the route. Underneath is a list of what happened at each stop, with the verse that tells it, for example "7. Philippi — Lydia baptised; Paul and Silas jailed; the earthquake (Acts 16:12)". Try `!journey paul 2`.
- 🗺️ **Eight journeys so far:** Abraham's journey from Ur (`!journey abraham`), the Exodus from Egypt to Jericho (`!journey exodus`), Paul's three missionary journeys (`!journey paul 1`, `paul 2`, `paul 3`), Paul's voyage and shipwreck on the way to Rome (`!journey rome`), the holy family's flight into Egypt (`!journey egypt`), and Jonah's (`!journey jonah`). `!journey` on its own lists them, and the menu under each map opens another.
- A place visited twice shows both numbers, as in "Antioch (1, 17)". A dashed ring means nobody is sure exactly where the place was, which is true of many stops on the Exodus.

## 2026-10-13 · Who Am I?, and taller family trees
- 🕵️ **New game: `!whoami`.** I think of someone from the Bible and give you one clue, a hard one: how many verses name them and where in the Bible they appear. Each press of **💡 Next clue** gives an easier one: the size of their family, where they were born and died, what their name means, a verse about them with the name hidden, their relatives' names, and finally the first letter of their name. Anyone in the channel can press **✍️ Guess**, one guess per clue each, and the first right answer wins. Other names count too, like "Israel" for Jacob or "Cephas" for Peter. **🏳️ Give up** shows who it was.
- 🌳 **Family trees now show far more ancestors.** `!tree` used to stop at the grandparents, which made Jesus's tree look tiny for a family the Bible traces back to Adam. Now the line of fathers climbs up the picture: great-grandfather, 2× great-grandfather, and so on for six generations. A box at the top says how much further it goes, such as "… 57 more generations, back to Adam". To see all of them, use `!tree Adam to Jesus`.
- 🩹 **`!who Peter` now finds Simon Peter.** Before, it said it didn't know anyone called Peter, because his full name is "Simon Peter". The same goes for others known by the second part of their name, like `!who Iscariot` or `!who Magdalene`.

## 2026-10-13 · Family trees
- 🌳 **New: `!tree` draws a family tree.** `!tree David` puts David in the middle of a picture of his family. His brothers and sisters are beside him, his wives next to him, and his children below (sons and daughters marked). His parents and grandparents are above, and across the top runs his line of fathers going back (… Judah › Pharez › … › Boaz › Obed › Jesse). In big families the best-known names come first: David had more than twenty children, and you'll see Solomon and Absalom before the rest.
- 📜 **Follow a line of descent:** `!tree Abraham to David` draws every generation from one person down to another as a chart, here all 14 from Abraham to David. `!tree Adam to Jesus` runs right through the Bible's genealogies, 65 generations. The names can go in either order, and `/tree` suggests names as you type.
- The family links come from the Bible's own genealogies and family records, so where the Bible doesn't say who someone's parents were, the tree stops there.

## 2026-10-13 · Psalm 119's lettered parts
- 🔤 **Psalm 119 now shows its 22 parts in `!metrical 119`.** Psalm 119 is an *acrostic*: in Hebrew it has 22 parts of eight verses, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and every verse in a part begins with that letter. Bibles print each letter as a heading (ALEPH, BETH, GIMEL…), and now the metrical psalm does too, for example **נ NUN · verses 105–112**. Each page keeps whole parts together, the page footer says which parts it holds, and a menu jumps straight to any of them.
- 🎯 **Ask for a part by its letter:** `!metrical 119 nun` gives the part that begins "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet".
- 🩹 **Fixed: verses 41–48 of Psalm 119 showed up twice** in `!metrical 119`. They now appear once.

## 2026-10-13 · Sing the Psalms: the Scottish Metrical Psalter
- 🎶 **New: `!metrical` — the Psalms set to sing.** A *metrical psalter* is the Book of Psalms rewritten in rhyme and a steady beat, so that a whole congregation can sing any psalm to a simple tune. The Scottish Psalter of 1650 is the most famous one; it's still sung in Scottish churches, and it gave us *The Lord's my shepherd, I'll not want*. All 150 psalms are here. Try `!metrical 23`. (`!psalm 23 sing` does the same thing.)
- ▶️ **Every psalm comes with its tune.** Each psalm shows the traditional tune it's sung to (Psalm 23 to *Crimond*, Psalm 100 to *Old Hundredth*) and who wrote it. Press **▶️ Play the tune** to hear one verse of it in four parts on an organ, then sing every stanza to it.
- 🎼 **Try it to a different tune.** Any tune with the same rhythm (the *meter*) fits a psalm's words, which is how these psalms have always been sung. The menu under each psalm plays its words' other possible tunes: Psalm 23 to *Dundee*, *St Anne*, *Martyrdom* and more. There are 27 tunes in all, from the 1500s to the 1800s.
- 🔢 **Stanzas are numbered by Bible verse**, as in the old psalm books, so you can see which verse each one comes from. Press **📖 Read in the KJV** to compare with the same psalm in ordinary prose.
- 📄 **Long psalms have page buttons**, or you can ask for just a few verses: `!metrical 119:105-112` ("Thy word is a lamp unto my feet").
- 🔁 **Some psalms have two versions.** The Psalter gives a few psalms a second version in a different rhythm. Psalm 100, for example, has a second version beginning "O all ye lands, unto the LORD". A button switches between them, or ask for one directly with `!metrical 100b`.
- 🔎 **Search by words or by tune.** `!metrical shepherd` finds psalms with that word, and `!metrical crimond` lists every psalm sung to *Crimond*. `!metrical` on its own picks one at random.

I toss three coins six times to build a "hexagram", a figure of six lines, then read you its Judgment and Image from James Legge's classic translation. Both new oracles have a **Card** button, and admins can choose them for the daily oracle (`!oraclesetup kind iching` or `hafez`).

## 2026-10-12 · Better Homer and Plato
- 📜 **Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* work with `!verse` now, in a better translation.** Looking up Homer used to fail with "has no chapter". Now you can look him up the way scholars cite him, by book and line: `!verse Iliad 6.440` shows that passage, `!verse Iliad 6.440-465` a longer stretch, and `!verse Odyssey 1` the whole first book, a page at a time. The text is now A. T. Murray's translation for the Loeb Classical Library, a close, line-by-line rendering of the Greek, in place of an older prose version that was labelled wrongly and had stray "[ ]" marks scattered through it. Hundreds of scanning errors in the *Iliad* have been corrected too (for example "Loerians" for Locrians).
- 🏛️ **Plato's dialogues are cut in the right places, and credit the right translators.** Plato is cited by "Stephanus numbers", the page-and-section numbers of a 1578 edition that every modern book prints in its margins: `!verse Republic 514a` is the start of the Allegory of the Cave. My sections used to run on into the next one, some were missing, and the *Laws* and the *Letters* couldn't be looked up at all: `!verse Laws 624a` said "not found". All of that works now. Every dialogue also names its actual translator (Fowler, Lamb, Bury or Shorey) instead of saying "Jowett" for all of them. Plato's *Apology* (Socrates' defence speech at his trial) was even credited to "Rufinus", the author of a different book with the same name.

## 2026-10-12 · The big update: notes, study buttons, new oracle & more
- 📝 **New command: `!notes`.** The NET Bible comes with thousands of notes from its translators explaining why a word was translated the way it was, what the original Greek or Hebrew says, and where ancient manuscripts disagree. Type `!notes John 3:16` to see them for any Bible verse (or up to 5 verses at once, like `!notes Genesis 1:1-3`). Small numbers in the verse show which word each note is about.

Each button works once per message.
